Rottweiler Teenage Phase: Behavior Changes From 6 to 24 Months and How to Train Through Them

Published: 2026-11-09 · Rottweiler Guide

The Rottweiler teenage phase typically begins around 6 months and lasts until 18–24 months, turning a once-reliable puppy into a dog who ignores cues, tests boundaries, and seems to forget everything he ever learned. Owners of adolescent Rottweilers often describe the same pattern: the treat that worked yesterday is suddenly boring, the leash that meant "let's go" now triggers a planted-stance debate, and the stranger who was fine last week is now barked at. You are not doing anything wrong, and your dog is not broken. This is a normal developmental stage driven by hormonal changes and brain remodeling, and how you handle these 12–18 months shapes the adult dog you will live with for the next decade.

TL;DR: The Rottweiler teenage phase brings selective hearing, testing of boundaries, and bursts of independent behavior. The solution is not stronger punishment or repeating cues louder. It is adjusting expectations, maintaining consistent rules across all handlers, and reinforcing the behaviors you want more deliberately than you did during puppyhood. The phase begins at 6–8 months, peaks between 9 and 14 months, and gradually resolves between 18 and 24 months as the dog matures mentally.

When Does the Rottweiler Teenage Phase Start and End?

The Rottweiler teenage phase begins around 6 months of age and can last until 18–24 months. This aligns with sexual maturity, which in Rottweilers occurs between 6 and 12 months, but the behavioral adolescence extends well beyond that because the brain continues to develop. A study of canine cognitive development found that the prefrontal cortex, which governs impulse control and decision-making, is one of the last brain regions to mature in dogs, similar to human adolescents.

According to the Wikipedia entry on the Rottweiler breed, males reach 65–80 lb by 6 months and 95–110 lb by 12 months, while females reach 55–70 lb at 6 months and 80–95 lb at 12 months. The physical growth is staggering, but the mental growth lags behind, which is why a 90-lb Rottweiler at 9 months still thinks and reacts like a puppy trapped in an adult body. The Rottweiler growth chart by month tracks this physical development in detail.

Key timeline milestones

  • 6–8 months: First signs of independence. Your Rottweiler may hesitate before obeying a cue he previously followed instantly.
  • 9–14 months: Peak of adolescent behavior. Testing boundaries, selective hearing, and hormonal influences are strongest here.
  • 15–18 months: Gradual improvement. The dog begins to settle but may still have regression periods.
  • 18–24 months: Mental maturity approaches. Most Rottweilers reach emotional stability somewhere in this window.

Why Do Rottweilers Change So Much During Adolescence?

Three major changes overlap during the teenage phase, and understanding each one helps you respond appropriately rather than taking the behavior personally.

Hormonal changes. Intact male Rottweilers experience a surge in testosterone between 6 and 12 months. This drives mounting, marking, roaming, and increased reactivity toward other male dogs. Females experience their first heat cycle between 6 and 12 months, which can cause moodiness, clinginess, or irritability. Neutering or spaying reduces some of these hormonal behaviors but does not eliminate the teenage phase entirely because the behavioral component is partly independent of hormones.

Brain remodeling. During adolescence, the dog's brain undergoes synaptic pruning, a process where unused neural connections are eliminated and frequently used ones are strengthened. This means behaviors you did not reinforce consistently during puppyhood are literally being erased from the dog's neural pathways. Everything you taught needs to be actively maintained through regular practice.

Fear period. Most dogs go through a second fear period during adolescence, typically between 9 and 14 months. Things your Rottweiler was fine with as a puppy, such as traffic, strangers, or novel objects, may suddenly trigger suspicion or fear. Unlike the puppy fear period that resolves quickly, the adolescent fear period can linger and shape lasting phobias if handled poorly.

Real-world scale: By 12 months, a male Rottweiler weighs 95–110 lb according to breed standard data posted on Wikipedia. At this weight, pulling, lunging, or bolting is not a minor nuisance but a genuine safety risk. This is why training during the teenage phase is not optional, it is a safety requirement.

How Do You Train Through the Rottweiler Teenage Phase?

The principle of growth mindset applies here: every ignored cue and every failed recall is data, not failure. Your job during this period is to adapt your training to the adolescent brain, not to force the adolescent brain to behave like an adult.

Reset your expectations

Expect your Rottweiler to perform at 60–70% of his puppyhood reliability during the peak adolescent months. This is normal. If he used to come when called 9 times out of 10, expect 6 out of 10 now. Plan your training sessions accordingly. Do not set up situations he will fail and then punish him for failing.

Go back to rewards

During puppyhood, you may have phased out food rewards for basic cues. Bring them back during the teenage phase. High-value rewards such as chicken, cheese, or liverwurst are not bribery, they are motivation for a brain that is distracted by hormones and environmental stimuli. Cue, reward, repeat. Once the phase passes, you can fade rewards again.

Increase the rate of reinforcement

In puppyhood, one treat every three correct responses may have worked. During adolescence, switch to one treat per correct response, especially for behaviors that compete with distractions. "Sit" in the living room needs less reinforcement than "sit" near a front door or a passing dog.

Prevent rehearsal of unwanted behaviors

Every time your adolescent Rottweiler practices bolting through a door, ignoring a recall, or jumping on a guest, he strengthens that neural pathway. Management is not cheating. Use a leash indoors, close doors before opening the front door, keep him behind a baby gate when visitors arrive, and use a long line for outdoor recall practice. You cannot train your way through a behavior that is being reinforced by repetition.

Strengthen engagement, not just obedience

Obedience cues like "sit" and "down" are important, but engagement is what keeps an adolescent dog connected to you. Practice attention games: look at me, hand targeting, and walking in heel position for a few steps in low-distraction environments. If you and your dog practice regularly in the yard, the Rottweiler enrichment ideas page has several toy-search and sniffing games that build engagement naturally.

Keep training sessions short

An adolescent Rottweiler has a shorter effective attention span than a well-trained adult. Three sessions of 3–5 minutes each day produce better results than a single 20-minute session. Quality over quantity applies especially during this phase.

What Are the Most Common Mistakes Owners Make?

Understanding what not to do is as important as knowing what to do. Professional dog trainer Will Atherton, whose YouTube video "Dog Training Mistakes In 'Teenage Phase'" has over 1.1 million views, emphasizes that the most common error is assuming the dog is being stubborn when the dog is simply processing competing drives.

Mistake 1: Repeating cues louder

When a teenage Rottweiler ignores a cue, the instinct is to say it louder and more sternly. This rarely works because the dog is not confused about the cue, he is weighing whether the reward for complying outweighs whatever is more interesting at that moment. Instead of repeating, wait a beat, move closer, or use a different reward to change the dog's motivation.

Mistake 2: Punishing growls and warnings

During the adolescent fear period, your Rottweiler may start growling at things he previously accepted. Punishing the growl removes the warning without removing the fear. The dog learns not to growl, but the fear remains and may escalate to a bite without warning. If your adolescent growls at a stranger or at handling, reduce the pressure and address the fear through counterconditioning, not punishment.

Mistake 3: Allowing jumping and mouthing to continue

Adolescent Rottweiler jumping is not a minor problem. An 80-lb dog launching at a visitor can knock over a child or an elderly person. If you allowed jumping during puppyhood because it was cute, stop it now. Turn away when he jumps, reward all four feet on the floor, and use a leash or baby gate to prevent rehearsal.

Mistake 4: Ending social walks

When the adolescent Rottweiler starts reacting to other dogs or strangers, many owners stop walks. This is the worst thing you can do. Instead, increase distance from triggers. Walk where you can see other dogs from 100 ft away, reward calm behavior, and gradually decrease distance over weeks. Isolation worsens reactivity because the dog never practices being calm around distractions.

How Do You Handle the Adolescent Fear Period?

The adolescent fear period is different from puppy fear because the triggers are often things the dog was previously fine with. A Rottweiler who loved car rides may suddenly refuse to get in. A dog who greeted children may start backing away.

Do not force exposure

Forcing your dog to confront a scary stimulus during the adolescent fear period can create a permanent phobia. If your Rottweiler is afraid of a particular street, walk another way for a week. If he is nervous about the car, practice just sitting in the parked car with treats, following the approach in the Rottweiler car loading guide, without driving anywhere.

Pair the scary thing with something good

See the trigger at a distance, feed a treat before the dog reacts, turn away before he reaches threshold. This is classical counterconditioning. Each successful pairing rewires the emotional response from fear to anticipation of something pleasant.

Watch for confidence gaps

Some Rottweilers who were bold puppies become cautious adolescents. This is especially common after a painful experience such as stepping on a sharp object, being startled by a loud noise, or having a negative interaction with another dog. Respect the confidence gap and build it back slowly through structured activities like trick training or nose work, where the dog controls the pace.

What About Neutering and Its Effect on the Teenage Phase?

Neutering a male Rottweiler reduces testosterone-driven behaviors such as mounting, roaming, and inter-dog aggression. However, it does not eliminate the teenage phase because many adolescent behaviors are not purely hormonal. A neutered adolescent can still ignore cues and test boundaries. The current veterinary consensus, reflected in major research databases, recommends waiting until the Rottweiler is at least 12–18 months old before neutering, especially for males, because early neutering is associated with a higher risk of orthopedic problems in large breeds. Discuss timing with your veterinarian, do not make the decision based solely on behavior.

Step-by-Step Weekly Training Plan for the Teenage Phase

This plan works as a frame you can adjust to your dog's specific challenges. The key is consistency across all seven days, not intensity on any single day.

Week 1: Foundation reset

Day 1–2: Practice "sit" and "down" in the kitchen with high-value food, no distractions. Return to rewarding every single correct response.

Day 3–4: Add mild distractions such as an open window or family member walking through the room. Reward generously for any response.

Day 5–7: Practice the same cues in the yard on a leash. If your dog ignores you, do not repeat. Move closer, get his attention, and reward compliance.

Week 2: Doorway manners and impulse control

Practice a "wait" at every door in the house. Start with the door closed, open it an inch, close it if he moves. Work up to the door fully open with the dog maintaining eye contact. Rottweilers are naturally territorial, and doorway bolting is one of the most common adolescent problems. This training directly addresses the guarding instinct in a controlled way.

Week 3: Structured walks with engagement

Walk your adolescent where the environment is boring, not exciting. The same quiet street every day for a week. Practice heeling for 10 steps, reward, release to sniff, recall, and repeat. Sniffing is calming, so let him sniff as his reward for heeling. Do not expect a loose leash for an entire walk. Allow sniff breaks after successful heeling segments.

Week 4: Real-world practice at a distance

By week four, you should have enough engagement to attempt training near a mild trigger at a distance. A quiet park where you can see another dog from across the field. Reward for looking at the trigger without reacting. Stay at this distance until your dog is consistently calm.

Frequently Asked Questions

At what age do Rottweilers calm down?

Most Rottweilers begin to show emotional stability between 18 and 24 months of age. The hyperactive puppy energy usually fades around 12–18 months, but mental maturity, which includes impulse control and reliable obedience, develops gradually through the second year. Some Rottweilers, especially working-line dogs, retain high drive well past 24 months but channel it more appropriately.

Is it normal for my 8-month-old Rottweiler to suddenly forget commands?

Yes. Around 6–9 months, the adolescent brain undergoes synaptic pruning, which can erase under-practiced behaviors. Your dog has not forgotten, he simply does not find the reward for complying as compelling as the adolescent impulses competing for his attention. Go back to rewarding every correct response with high-value food and keep training sessions short.

Why is my Rottweiler suddenly aggressive toward other dogs?

Social maturity in Rottweilers often brings same-sex aggression, especially in males between 12 and 24 months. This is not true aggression in many cases but rather a change in social tolerance. Management, distance, and careful introductions are the first line. Do not force interactions with unfamiliar dogs during this period.

Should I stop taking my Rottweiler to the dog park during adolescence?

Many adolescent Rottweilers are not good candidates for dog parks. As they mature socially, they may correct other dogs harshly or be targeted by other dogs because of their size and breed. If you do go, go at off-hours, keep the session short, and leave immediately if your dog shows any signs of stress or conflict. Structured one-on-one play with known dogs is safer than open-group play.

How long does the Rottweiler fear period last?

The adolescent fear period typically lasts 2–4 weeks but can extend longer if the trigger is severe or the dog's response is reinforced by handler reaction. If your dog seems afraid of something new, do not force exposure. Countercondition with high-value food at a safe distance and the fear period usually resolves faster than if you push through it.

Will neutering fix my Rottweiler's teenage behavior?

Neutering reduces some testosterone-driven behaviors such as mounting and roaming, but it does not eliminate adolescent testing of boundaries, selective hearing, or fear period reactions. Most behaviorists recommend neutering after 12–18 months for large breeds and managing adolescent behavior through training regardless of neuter status.

My Rottweiler resource guards his food bowl during adolescence, what do I do?

Do not punish the growl or take the bowl away. Trade up: offer something better than the food bowl, such as a piece of chicken or a stuffed Kong, while he is eating. Let him see you add treats to his bowl throughout the meal. If the behavior is severe or has escalated to snapping, work with a certified behavior professional. Do not test the dog by reaching for his bowl.

How do I stop my adolescent Rottweiler from jumping on visitors?

Keep a leash on your dog when guests arrive. Step on the leash so he cannot jump. Ask him to sit, and reward the sit. Have guests ignore him until all four feet are on the floor. If he has already learned that jumping works, you need to prevent the behavior entirely for 2–3 weeks while rewarding the alternative behavior (sitting or going to a mat).

What to Expect Next

The Rottweiler teenage phase is a test of patience and consistency, but it is finite. Every day you maintain your rules through management and reinforcement, you build the neural pathways your adult dog will rely on for the rest of his life. By the time your Rottweiler reaches 24 months, the effort you put in now will pay off in a confident, reliable, and calm adult companion. If you are struggling with a specific behavior, the Rottweiler Guide blog has detailed articles on leash training, enrichment, and car loading that address the most common adolescent challenges. You do not need to fix everything at once. Pick one behavior, work on it for a week, and trust the process.
