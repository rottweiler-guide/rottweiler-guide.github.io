Rottweiler Teenage Phase: Behavior Changes From 6 to 24 Months and How to Train Through Them

Published: 2026-11-08 · Rottweiler Guide

The Rottweiler teenage phase hits somewhere between 6 and 10 months, and in the span of a single week your steady, biddable puppy can turn into a 70-lb adolescent who stares through you, ignores a cue he knew at 4 months, and has opinions about everything from the leash to the furniture. It is not regression, it is not defiance, and it is certainly not a sign you failed. It is a normal, well-documented developmental window in large-breed dogs, and with the right framework it passes without wrecking your training.

TL;DR: The Rottweiler teenage phase runs from roughly 6 to 24 months, with the most dramatic behavioral testing between 8 and 16 months. During this window, hormonal surges and brain restructuring temporarily override learned behaviors. Your job is not to punish the teen into submission or go back to puppy basics — it is to stay consistent, reward the behaviors you want, manage the environment so failure is not an option, and outlast the phase. What Is the Rottweiler Teenage Phase?

Canine adolescence is the period between sexual maturity and social maturity, and in large, slow-maturing breeds like the Rottweiler it lasts longer than in small dogs. Research on canine development has mapped this period as roughly 6 to 24 months depending on breed size, with the most pronounced behavioral upheaval occurring in the middle. A 2010 study published in Applied Animal Behaviour Science found that dogs between 6 and 18 months showed a significant drop in obedience — the same dogs who had reliably performed cues at 5 months were ignoring them at 9 months, even without any change in training (Asher et al., 2010). The study linked this not to poor training but to brain restructuring during adolescence, similar to the rewiring that happens in human teenagers.

For Rottweilers specifically, the phase is stretched out. The FCI breed standard (FCI Standard No. 147) describes the temperament as "good-natured, placid in basic disposition" and "self-assured." That self-assurance, which you chose the breed for, is exactly what makes a Rottweiler teen harder to manage than a Golden Retriever teen. A placid, confident dog does not run away from a conflict — he stands his ground, considers your request, and sometimes decides it is not worth his time.

When Does the Rottweiler Teenage Phase Start and End?

Most Rottweilers enter adolescence between 6 and 9 months and do not fully settle until 20 to 24 months. The exact timing varies by individual, but the pattern is predictable enough to plan around.

If your Rottweiler was neutered early, you may see a slightly different hormonal trajectory — research in large breeds suggests early neutering can alter behavioral development patterns, though the adolescent window itself still exists. Discuss the timing of neutering with your veterinarian.

Why Does My Rottweiler Teenager Ignore Commands He Knew at 4 Months?

Because his brain is physically pruning synapses. During adolescence, the canine brain undergoes a process called synaptic pruning — the same thing human brains do during the teen years. Less-used neural connections are stripped away to make room for more efficient adult wiring. That means some previously reliable behaviors briefly disappear as the brain reorders itself. It is not forgetting; it is remodeling.

The same Applied Animal Behaviour Science study noted that dogs in the 6-18 month window were significantly less likely to respond to known cues in distracting environments even when they remained perfectly capable of performing them in a quiet room. The difference was context: the dog knew the cue but could not access the memory in a high-distraction setting. That is not a training failure; it is a developmental constraint.

Trainer Will Atherton, who has worked with hundreds of adolescent dogs including Rottweilers, describes this as "a perfect puppy who suddenly seems to forget everything" and notes that the most common owner mistake is assuming the dog needs more punishment when what he actually needs is clearer structure and lower stakes (Will Atherton, Surviving the Teenage Phase).

How to handle the command gaps

Drop your expectations for reliability in public until the dog is past 16 months. Keep practicing in low-distraction settings, reward heavily for even partial compliance, and do not repeat a cue more than twice — if he does not respond, reset and help him succeed (shorter distance, fewer distractions) rather than letting him practice ignoring you.

Fear Periods During Adolescence: Why Your Rottweiler Is Suddenly Nervous

Rottweilers go through at least two fear periods as puppies, and adolescence can trigger a third one. Between 12 and 18 months, some previously confident young Rottweilers suddenly hesitate at things they used to ignore — a trash can on the sidewalk, a new surface, an unfamiliar person.

This is normal and temporary. The key rule: never force a dog past something he is wary of during a fear period, because you will confirm that the thing really is dangerous. Instead, let him approach at his own pace, feed treats calmly near the trigger, and avoid making a big deal of the moment. If your teen Rottweiler suddenly refuses to enter the car or balks at a doorway, see the step-by-step protocol in our guide on loading a reluctant Rottweiler into the car — the same principle of threshold desensitization applies.

How to Train a Rottweiler Teenager: Phase-by-Phase Plan

6-9 months: Prevent the habits before they stick

This is the window where behavior problems start, not where they peak. Your goal is to prevent practice of unwanted behaviors. Manage the environment so the dog cannot rehearse jumping on counters, bolting out doors, or pulling to greet every dog. Use baby gates, a crate, and a short house line. If you have not been rewarding a solid "leave it" and a recall that works in the living room, build those now, before the teenage drive to explore overpowers your voice.

9-14 months: Hold the line and lower the criteria

This is the peak. Your carefully trained 6-month-old looks like a stranger. Do not escalate. If he blows off a recall, do not call him again; go get him on the long line and reset. If he pulls on leash, stop moving, do not yank. Reward any check-in, any slack in the leash, any moment of eye contact. The leash-planting plan from this blog uses the same principles and works for the adolescent dog who suddenly stops cooperating on walks.

Keep training sessions very short — two to three minutes, several times a day. Higher-value rewards than you used from 2 to 6 months are not bribes; they are fair compensation for a dog who is suddenly much more interested in the world.

14-18 months: Exploit the good days

Progress comes in fits. You will have a perfect walk one day and a disaster the next. On the good days, push a little: practice a recall in a new location, work on a down-stay with mild distractions. On the bad days, go back to what worked at 10 months — short sessions, low distraction, high reward. This is not a setback; it is consolidation.

18-24 months: Build the adult dog

Most Rottweilers begin to settle noticeably after 18 months. The brain has finished its major pruning, hormones have stabilized, and the dog who gave you gray hair at 12 months is starting to feel like a partner. Now is the time to generalize all the skills you have been maintaining: take training to dog-friendly stores, busy parks, sidewalks with traffic. Your Rottweiler is ready for real-world reliability in a way he was not at 10 months. The Rottweiler growth chart by month confirms that physical maturity aligns with this behavioral settling — by 20-24 months the dog is at or near his adult weight and his joints are ready for more structured exercise like jogging or hiking.

How to Manage Exercise for a Teenage Rottweiler

A teenager who does not get enough physical and mental outlet will invent his own, and you probably will not like what he invents. But there is a limit: a Rottweiler's growth plates do not close until 18-24 months, so forced running, repetitive jumping, and agility work can damage developing joints.

The sweet spot is structured physical exercise plus plenty of mental work. For a 10-month-old Rottweiler, that means two 20-minute leash walks, a short training session, 10 minutes of tug or fetch on soft ground, and a food puzzle or Kong for mental work. Do not jog or run with him until a vet confirms growth plates are closed. If you need low-impact ideas that also build his brain, check the enrichment ideas guide — scent games, trick training, and puzzle toys exercise the dog without stressing his skeleton.

What Mistakes Make the Teenage Phase Worse?

  • Repeating a cue he is ignoring. Every repetition he ignores is practice for ignoring. Say it once, then help him succeed.
  • Punishing the "shut down." If your dog looks worried when you correct him, you are pushing too hard. Back off and use rewards, not force.
  • Doubling down on corrections. An adolescent Rottweiler who is pushed will push back harder. A firm "no" and a reset work better than escalating.
  • Stopping training during the bad weeks. The worst time to stop is when he is hardest to train. Short sessions, every day, even when they feel pointless.
  • Comparing him to his puppy self. He is not broken; he is growing. The puppy behavior is still in there; it will come back when his brain catches up.
  • Letting him rehearse unwanted behaviors on walks. Every pull, every lunge, every ignored recall is learning. Use a long line, manage the environment, set him up to win.

Does Neutering Help or Hurt a Rottweiler Teenager?

The relationship between neutering and adolescent behavior is more complicated than most owners realize. The 2022 Frontiers in Veterinary Science study already cited found that early neutering (before 12 months) in large breeds was linked to a higher incidence of certain behavioral issues including fearfulness and reactivity — the opposite of what many owners expect. The study joins a growing body of evidence that delaying neutering until after 18-24 months may produce a more behaviorally stable adult dog, especially in breeds predisposed to orthopedic issues and certain cancers.

This is a decision to make with your veterinarian, not as a quick fix for teenage behavior. Neutering will not teach your dog to walk on a loose leash or stop jumping on visitors. It may reduce some hormone-driven behaviors like urine marking or roaming, but the foundation of a well-behaved adult Rottweiler is training, not surgery.

Checklist for Surviving the Rottweiler Teenage Phase

  • Short training sessions (2-3 minutes) spread throughout the day, not one long session
  • Management over punishment: crates, house lines, baby gates, long lines
  • Reward check-ins and slack leash on every walk
  • Never repeat a cue more than twice; reset if he does not respond
  • One high-value reward session per day (cheese, liver, or boiled chicken — not kibble)
  • Daily mental enrichment: Kong, sniff mat, trick training, scent games
  • Structured exercise on soft ground, not forced running
  • Accept that you will have bad days; reset tomorrow
  • Track one behavior per week and count small wins (sustained eye contact, a loose-leash minute, a recall in the yard)
  • Consult your vet about neutering timing; do not use it as a behavioral shortcut

Frequently Asked Questions

At what age do Rottweilers become teenagers?

Most enter adolescence between 6 and 9 months, with the hardest period typically falling between 9 and 14 months. The phase lasts until about 18-24 months in large, slow-maturing breeds.

Why has my Rottweiler suddenly forgotten his training?

Canine adolescence involves brain restructuring (synaptic pruning) that temporarily disrupts access to previously learned behaviors. He has not forgotten; his brain needs time to rewire. Keep training short and low-distraction, and reward generously.

Does neutering calm a teenage Rottweiler?

Not reliably. Recent research links early neutering to increased fearfulness and reactivity in some large breeds. Neutering may reduce hormone-driven behaviors like roaming but will not teach impulse control or loose-leash walking. Discuss timing with your vet.

How much exercise does an adolescent Rottweiler need?

Two 20-minute leash walks plus structured play and mental enrichment are enough. Avoid forced running and high-impact jumping until growth plates close (18-24 months). A tired teenager is a good teenager, but physical fatigue should come from brain work, not joint stress.

Why is my Rottweiler suddenly scared of things he used to ignore?

A second fear period can hit during adolescence, usually between 12 and 18 months. Never force him past something he is wary of. Let him approach triggers at his own pace, reward calm behavior near them, and wait for the phase to pass.

When does the Rottweiler teenage phase end?

Most Rottweilers begin to settle noticeably around 18 to 20 months and are significantly more reliable by 24 months. Some larger males may still show flashes of adolescent behavior until 2.5 years, but the intensity drops sharply after 18 months.

Should I use a prong collar or e-collar on my teenage Rottweiler?

The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior (AVSAB) recommends against aversive tools for training, particularly during adolescence when dogs are more sensitive to negative experiences. Reward-based methods with good management are safer and produce more reliable long-term results.

Is leash reactivity normal during the teenage phase?

Many adolescent dogs develop temporary leash frustration or excitement that looks like reactivity. Manage it with distance, high-value rewards for calm behavior near triggers, and short sessions. If it escalates or does not improve within a few weeks, consult a trainer.

The Short Version

A Rottweiler between 6 and 24 months will test you in ways his puppy self never did — and that is normal. The brain is rewiring, hormones are spiking, and the grown-up body has arrived before the grown-up impulse control. You do not need better tools, harsher corrections, or a different dog. You need consistency, management, lowered expectations for reliability in public, and the patience to outlast a developmental window that every large-breed owner goes through. Track your week with the checklist above, use the stage checker to guide your focus, and if you hit a genuinely bad patch, revisit the other practical guides on this blog for the specific skill that has gone missing. This phase ends. The adult dog he will become is worth the wait.
